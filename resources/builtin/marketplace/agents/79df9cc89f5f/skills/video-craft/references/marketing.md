# Advertising and marketing videos

Read for an ad, promo, launch, brand or campaign video, including a service or
business with no single product to show, and for versions made to test or to
run in several placements. Selling a specific product also follows
[ecommerce.md](ecommerce.md); a product, app or site with a page follows
[product-source.md](product-source.md).

## The brief

- Settle four things from the brief and what can be found: who it is for, where
  it runs, what the viewer should feel or do afterwards, and what the business
  can truthfully stand behind (offers, results, customers, credentials). Ask
  only for what would change the idea.
- Write one design read: what the video should feel like and why, tied to the
  audience and placement ("calm and credible, for clinic owners scrolling a
  feed"), not adjectives alone ("modern and clean"). Let it set the look, pace
  and music.

## The idea

- One idea per video, in one sentence: a truth the audience feels, met by what
  this business actually does, shown as something a camera can film. If it
  needs two sentences, it is two ads.
- Test it by swapping in a competitor's logo. If the video still works, the idea
  is not specific yet. Reach for this business's own materials, places, offer
  and, best of all, its customers' own words.
- With nothing to demonstrate (a brand, a service, a cause, a new idea), show the
  outcome in people and places, a before and after the audience recognises, or
  one visual metaphor carried through the whole video. The feeling comes from
  what is on screen, not from adjectives; the name and promise land on the end
  card.
- When only a few shots can be generated, carry them through to the end: a
  later beat can use another moment of a clip, a tighter crop or a slow push on
  one of its frames under the words. A run of plain text cards over a flat
  background reads as slides; let only the end card stand alone.
- Ask for stills the page can move: wider than the frame with the subject clear
  of the edges, so it can be pushed toward or panned across, or two states or
  angles of the subject to cut between. One key image cannot carry the whole ad.
- Give each still one move that shows what its line points at: push toward the
  detail the line names, pan from one thing to the next, or cut to a tighter
  crop on the beat. A picture that only sits while the words change reads as a
  slide; the end card can hold still.
- Cast for the arc: someone the viewer wants to become for an aspirational
  piece, someone they recognise as themselves for a problem piece.

## Shape

Choose the arc the material supports; these are options, not a sequence to fill:

- Problem, then answer, when the audience already feels the pain. Reveal the
  answer about a third of the way in, so the relief has contrast.
- Vision, then proof, for a new kind of product. Name it early so the vision has
  an anchor.
- Before, after and the bridge between them, when the promise is a better way
  of working.
- Question and instant demonstration, repeated, when seeing it is the argument.
- A quick run of features, each with what it does for the viewer, when desire
  rather than pain drives the choice.
- One person's story (founder, customer, a day in the work), when trust or
  identity sells.

Familiar social formats (review, unboxing, tutorial, try-on, live-stream clip)
each have one job; see the formats in [ecommerce.md](ecommerce.md). A person
talking to camera follows [presenter.md](presenter.md).

Open with the strongest hook the material supports: the audience's problem said
back to them, a credible number, a direct call to who it is for, an "imagine"
line, a question, or a striking image.

## Writing

- For each beat, know what persuades: a demonstration, a specific number, other
  customers, a risk removed, a price anchor, chaos turning calm. "Show benefits"
  is not a mechanism.
- Write lines a person would say, about a particular person: "book the 7 a.m.
  slot from the bus stop" beats "streamline your scheduling". Avoid strings of
  noun phrases and claims any company could make.
- Answer the one or two doubts most likely to stop the viewer (fit, effort,
  quality, price, what happens after buying) with something they can see or a
  stated fact.
- End on one action: what to do, where, and what they get. Hold the end card
  long enough to read, with the real logo and name.

## Proof and claims

- What the product does can be stated. Numbers, ratings, rankings, comparisons,
  customer quotes, before and after, and endorsements need a source the user or
  the page gave, quoted exactly, not rounded up. Without one, leave them out or
  turn them into a question or a scene. Offers, prices, trials and deadlines are
  never invented.
- A real customer, the founder or a real event is the claim itself: use the
  user's footage or leave it out. A generated person is not a customer and a
  generated scene is not a result; never present either as a testimonial or a
  measurement.
- Ad platforms reject copy that asserts the viewer's personal situation
  ("Struggling with debt?"). Speak about the product or the outcome instead.

## Versions for testing and placements

- Make one strong main cut first, then derive versions: copy `videos/main` to
  `videos/<id>` for each and change only what differs. Footage and voice are
  shared, so a version costs no new generation unless it needs a new shot.
- Versions that look alike teach little; platforms tend to treat them as one ad.
  First test different angles, one hook each (the pain, a surprising fact, the
  proof, a contrast), then vary the wording of the angle that wins.
- Keep the look, product and everything else fixed and change one thing in
  each, roughly in order of effect: the hook line with its opening image, the
  proof, the call to action, the length, the ratio. Name each by what changed
  (`hook-proof`, `cut-15s`).
- Re-edit a shorter cut rather than trimming it: keep the hook, the strongest
  proof and the action; a 6-second cut carries one message.
- Recompose a new ratio in the page rather than cropping or padding the finished
  cut. Taller: hook near
  the top, subject in the middle, offer above the bottom band the app covers.
  Wider: subject on one side, words on the other, fewer of them. Use fewer words
  rather than smaller type.
- Common shapes: 9:16 for TikTok, Reels and Shorts; 4:5 or 1:1 for feeds; 16:9
  for YouTube, where the hook has to land before the skip button at five
  seconds. Check the current spec when the user names a placement.
- In the reply, list the versions and what each one tests.

## Review

From the frames, answer: who is it for, what is the one idea, what proves it,
and what should the viewer do? Does it still work with the sound off? Is the
product shown working, or only shown? Do people and places stay on screen to
the end, or does it turn into slides? Could the beats be shuffled without
loss? Then it is a set of shots, not an ad. Would it still work with another
company's logo on it? Then sharpen the idea.
